§ Proofs — Mean Value Theorems

Rolle's Theorem — Proof

If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(f(a)=f(b)\), then \(\exists c\in(a,b)\) s.t. \(f'(c)=0\).

Proof

Step 1 If \(f\) is constant: \(f'=0\) everywhere; take any \(c\).

Step 2 If \(f\) is not constant: since \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), by Extreme Value Theorem it attains its max \(M\) and min \(m\). Since \(f\) is non-constant and \(f(a)=f(b)\), at least one of \(M,m\) is not attained at the endpoints.

Step 3 Say \(f(c)=M\) with \(c\in(a,b)\). Then \(f'(c)\) exists (given) and \(c\) is an interior maximum.

Step 4 At interior maximum: \(f'(c^+)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\le0\) and \(f'(c^-)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\ge0\). Since \(f'(c)\) exists: \(f'(c)=f'(c^+)=f'(c^-)\Rightarrow f'(c)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem — Proof

If \(f,g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) are continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(g'(x)\ne0\) on \((a,b)\), then \(\exists c\in(a,b)\) s.t. \(\dfrac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}\).

Proof

Define \(h(x)=f(x)-\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}g(x)\). Then \(h(a)=f(a)-\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}g(a)\) and \(h(b)=f(b)-\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}g(b)\). Verify: \(h(a)=h(b)\) iff \(f(a)-f(b)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}(g(a)-g(b))\), which simplifies to \(1=1\). So \(h(a)=h(b)\). Apply Rolle to \(h\): \(\exists c\) with \(h'(c)=0\Rightarrow f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}g'(c)\). \(\blacksquare\)

L'Hôpital's Rule — Proof (0/0 case)
Proof via Cauchy MVT

Let \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=0\), \(\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0\), and \(\lim_{x\to a}f'(x)/g'(x)=L\). Define \(f(a)=g(a)=0\). For \(x\ne a\): apply Cauchy MVT to \([a,x]\) (or \([x,a]\)): \(\exists c\) between \(a\) and \(x\) s.t. \(\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}=\dfrac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}\). As \(x\to a\): \(c\to a\), so \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\dfrac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}\to L\). \(\blacksquare\)

§ Proofs — Taylor's Theorem & Asymptotes

Taylor's Theorem with Cauchy Remainder — Proof

\(f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n\), where \(R_n=\dfrac{(x-a)^n}{n!}\cdot\dfrac{(x-c)^{1-p}}{p}\cdot f^{(n+1)}(c)\) (Cauchy form) or \(R_n=\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\) (Lagrange form).

Proof of Lagrange Remainder

Fix \(x\) and define \(F(t)=f(x)-\sum_{k=0}^n\dfrac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}(x-t)^k\) and \(G(t)=(x-t)^{n+1}\). Note \(F(x)=0,G(x)=0\). Apply Cauchy MVT to \([a,x]\): \(\dfrac{F(a)-F(x)}{G(a)-G(x)}=\dfrac{F'(\xi)}{G'(\xi)}\). Compute \(F'(t)\): most terms cancel (telescope), leaving \(F'(t)=-\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(t)}{n!}(x-t)^n\). \(G'(t)=-(n+1)(x-t)^n\). So \(\dfrac{F(a)}{(x-a)^{n+1}}=\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\Rightarrow R_n=\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Euler's Theorem on Homogeneous Functions — Proof

If \(f(x,y)\) is homogeneous of degree \(n\): \(x\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}+y\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=nf\).

Proof

By definition: \(f(tx,ty)=t^n f(x,y)\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\): \(x\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial(tx)}\cdot1+y\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial(ty)}\cdot1=nt^{n-1}f(x,y)\). Put \(t=1\): \(x\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}+y\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=nf(x,y)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Extension (2nd order): \(x^2f_{xx}+2xyf_{xy}+y^2f_{yy}=n(n-1)f\). Proof: differentiate Euler's relation w.r.t. \(x\) and apply Euler again.

§ Proofs — Functions of Several Variables

Second Derivative Test for Two Variables — Proof Sketch

At a critical point \((a,b)\): let \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\). If \(D>0,f_{xx}>0\): local min; \(D>0,f_{xx}<0\): local max; \(D<0\): saddle.

Proof (via Taylor expansion)

Near \((a,b)\): \(f(a+h,b+k)\approx f(a,b)+\dfrac{1}{2}(f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2)\) (critical point: first partials = 0). The quadratic form \(Q(h,k)=f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2\) has signature determined by \(D=\det\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{xy}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}\). If \(D>0\): \(Q\) is positive or negative definite (determined by sign of \(f_{xx}\)) → local extremum. If \(D<0\): \(Q\) indefinite → saddle. \(\blacksquare\)

Lagrange Multipliers — Proof

At a constrained extremum of \(f\) subject to \(g=0\): \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) for some \(\lambda\).

Proof (Implicit Function Theorem approach)

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) has extremum at \((a,b)\) subject to \(g(x,y)=0\). Near \((a,b)\), parameterize the constraint curve: \(x=x(t),y=y(t)\) with \(\dot x(0)=\dot x,\dot y(0)=\dot y\). The constraint gives \(g_x\dot x+g_y\dot y=0\). The function along the curve: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t))\big|_{t=0}=f_x\dot x+f_y\dot y=0\) at extremum. So \(\vec\nabla f\cdot\vec{v}=0\) for all tangent vectors \(\vec v\) to the constraint. Since \(\nabla g\cdot\vec v=0\) defines the same orthogonal complement: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\). \(\blacksquare\)

Jacobian and Change of Variables in Double Integrals — Proof

\(\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\iint_S f(x(u,v),y(u,v))\left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\,du\,dv\).

Proof (Geometric)

Under the map \((u,v)\mapsto(x,y)\): a small rectangle \([u,u+\Delta u]\times[v,v+\Delta v]\) maps to a parallelogram with sides \(\vec a=\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial u}\Delta u\) and \(\vec b=\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial v}\Delta v\). Area of parallelogram = \(|\vec a\times\vec b|=\left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\Delta u\Delta v\). Summing: \(dA=|J|\,du\,dv\). \(\blacksquare\)

§ Proofs — Beta & Gamma Functions

\(\Gamma(n+1)=n\Gamma(n)\) — Proof
Proof (Integration by Parts)

\(\Gamma(n+1)=\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,dt\). Integrate by parts: \(u=t^n,dv=e^{-t}dt\): \([{-t^ne^{-t}}]_0^\infty+n\int_0^\infty t^{n-1}e^{-t}\,dt=0+n\Gamma(n)=n\Gamma(n)\). \(\blacksquare\)

\(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi\) — Proof
Proof (Polar coordinates)

\(I=\Gamma(1/2)=\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}e^{-t}\,dt\). Substitute \(t=x^2\): \(I=2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx\). So \(I^2=4\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy\). Convert to polar: \(I^2=4\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta=4\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}=\pi\). Hence \(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

\(B(m,n)=\dfrac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}\) — Proof
Proof

\(\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)=\int_0^\infty x^{m-1}e^{-x}\,dx\cdot\int_0^\infty y^{n-1}e^{-y}\,dy=\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty x^{m-1}y^{n-1}e^{-(x+y)}\,dx\,dy\).

Substitute \(x=st,y=s(1-t)\) (so \(x+y=s\), \(x/(x+y)=t\)): Jacobian = \(s\). Range: \(s\in(0,\infty),t\in(0,1)\).

\(=\int_0^\infty\int_0^1(st)^{m-1}(s(1-t))^{n-1}e^{-s}\cdot s\,dt\,ds=\int_0^\infty s^{m+n-1}e^{-s}\,ds\int_0^1 t^{m-1}(1-t)^{n-1}\,dt=\Gamma(m+n)B(m,n)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Walli's Formula (derived from Beta function)

\(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^m\theta\cos^n\theta\,d\theta=\dfrac{B\!\left(\dfrac{m+1}{2},\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right)}{2}=\dfrac{\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{m+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right)}{2\,\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{m+n+2}{2}\right)}\).

Special: \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n\theta\,d\theta=\dfrac{(n-1)!!}{n!!}\cdot\begin{cases}\pi/2&n\text{ even}\\1&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}\).

§ Proofs — Multiple Integrals

Fubini's Theorem (for continuous functions)

If \(f(x,y)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\times[c,d]\): \(\iint f\,dA=\int_a^b\!\left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx=\int_c^d\!\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy\).

Change of Order Strategy

Draw the region \(R\). Then: (1) Describe \(R\) with \(x\) first → do \(dx\) first. (2) Describe with \(y\) first → do \(dy\) first. Equality of both = Fubini.

PYQ 2023 — Leibniz nth Derivative Formula

Find the \(n\)th derivative of \(f(x)=e^{2x}\sin 3x\).

Solution

Write \(e^{2x}\sin3x=\text{Im}(e^{(2+3i)x})\). \(\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{(2+3i)x}=(2+3i)^ne^{(2+3i)x}\). Now \(|2+3i|=\sqrt{13}\), \(\arg(2+3i)=\alpha=\arctan(3/2)\). So \((2+3i)^n=(\sqrt{13})^n e^{in\alpha}\). Thus \(f^{(n)}(x)=13^{n/2}e^{2x}\sin(3x+n\alpha)\) where \(\alpha=\arctan(3/2)\).

PYQ 2022 — Rolle's Application

Show that between any two real roots of \(e^x\sin x=1\), there is at least one root of \(e^x\cos x+1=0\).

Solution

Let \(f(x)=e^x\sin x-1\). Suppose \(f(\alpha)=f(\beta)=0\) with \(\alpha<\beta\). By Rolle's theorem: \(\exists c\in(\alpha,\beta)\) with \(f'(c)=0\). \(f'(x)=e^x\sin x+e^x\cos x=e^x(\sin x+\cos x)\). At \(c\): \(e^c(\sin c+\cos c)=0\Rightarrow\sin c+\cos c=0\Rightarrow\tan c=-1\). But at \(c\): \(f(c)\ne0\) in general. Actually: \(f'(x)=e^x(\sin x+\cos x)=0\Rightarrow\sin x+\cos x=0\Rightarrow\cos x=-\sin x\Rightarrow e^x\cos x=-e^x\sin x=-(1)=-1\Rightarrow e^c\cos c+1=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

PYQ 2018 — Asymptotes

Find all asymptotes of \(y(1+x^2)=x\).

Solution

Rewrite: \(y=\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\). As \(x\to\pm\infty\): \(y\to0\). So \(y=0\) is a horizontal asymptote. No vertical asymptotes (denominator \(1+x^2>0\) always). No oblique asymptotes (since \(y\to0<\infty\)). Only asymptote: \(y=0\).

Find the equation of the tangent and normal to the curve \(x=t^2,y=t^3\) at the point \(t=2\).

Solution

At \(t=2\): \((x,y)=(4,8)\). \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\dfrac{3t^2}{2t}=\dfrac{3t}{2}\bigg|_{t=2}=3\). Tangent: \(y-8=3(x-4)\Rightarrow y=3x-4\). Normal: \(y-8=-\dfrac{1}{3}(x-4)\Rightarrow 3y+x=28\).

Find oblique asymptotes of \(y=\dfrac{x^3}{(x-1)^2}\).

PYQ 2015 — Envelope of Family of Curves

§ PYQ — Maxima-Minima & Lagrange (2000–2024)

PYQ 2024 — Maxima-Minima of Two Variables

Find the maxima and minima of \(f(x,y)=x^3+y^3-3xy\).

Solution

Critical points: \(f_x=3x^2-3y=0\Rightarrow y=x^2\). \(f_y=3y^2-3x=0\Rightarrow x=y^2\). So \(x=y^2=x^4\Rightarrow x(x^3-1)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(x=1\). Points: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\).

\(f_{xx}=6x,f_{yy}=6y,f_{xy}=-3\). \(D=36xy-9\). At \((0,0)\): \(D=-9<0\) → saddle. At \((1,1)\): \(D=36-9=27>0,f_{xx}=6>0\) → local minimum with \(f(1,1)=-1\).

PYQ 2022 — Lagrange (on Ellipse)

Find the maximum and minimum distances from the origin to the ellipse \(3x^2+4xy+3y^2=1\).

Solution

Minimize \(f=x^2+y^2\) subject to \(g=3x^2+4xy+3y^2-1=0\). Lagrange: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\): \(2x=\lambda(6x+4y)\), \(2y=\lambda(4x+6y)\). This gives \(\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&6\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{2}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\). Eigenvalues of \(\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&6\end{pmatrix}\): \(\lambda_1=10,\lambda_2=2\). So \(2/\lambda=10\to\lambda=1/5\) or \(2/\lambda=2\to\lambda=1\). From \(g=1\): at \(\lambda=1/5\): \((x,y)=k(1,1)/\sqrt2\), \(3+4+3=10k^2=1\Rightarrow k=1/\sqrt{10}\Rightarrow r^2=2k^2=1/5\). At \(\lambda=1\): \((x,y)=k(1,-1)/\sqrt2\), \(3-4+3=2k^2=1\Rightarrow k=1/\sqrt2\Rightarrow r^2=1\). Min distance \(=1/\sqrt5\), max distance \(=1\).

PYQ 2021 — Lagrange (Ellipsoid)

Find the maximum of \(f=xyz\) subject to \(x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2=1\).

Solution

Lagrange: \(yz=\lambda(2x/a^2)\), \(xz=\lambda(2y/b^2)\), \(xy=\lambda(2z/c^2)\). Multiply each by \(x,y,z\) respectively: \(xyz=2\lambda x^2/a^2=2\lambda y^2/b^2=2\lambda z^2/c^2\). So \(x^2/a^2=y^2/b^2=z^2/c^2=1/3\) (summing to 1). Thus \(x=a/\sqrt3,y=b/\sqrt3,z=c/\sqrt3\). Maximum value \(f=\dfrac{abc}{3\sqrt3}\).

PYQ 2019 — Saddle Point

Show that \(f(x,y)=x^3-3xy^2\) has neither max nor min at the origin but has a saddle point.

Solution

At \((0,0)\): \(f_x=3x^2-3y^2=0\), \(f_y=-6xy=0\). Critical point. \(f_{xx}=6x=0,f_{yy}=-6x=0,f_{xy}=-6y=0\). \(D=0\): test inconclusive.

Along \(y=0\): \(f(x,0)=x^3>0\) for \(x>0\) and \(<0\) for \(x<0\). So \(f\) takes both positive and negative values near origin → saddle point. \(\blacksquare\)

PYQ 2016 — Box of Maximum Volume

Find the dimensions of the rectangular box of maximum volume inscribed in a sphere of radius \(R\).

Solution

Volume \(V=8xyz\), constraint \(x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2\). Lagrange: \(8yz=2\lambda x\), \(8xz=2\lambda y\), \(8xy=2\lambda z\). Multiply by \(x,y,z\): \(8xyz=2\lambda x^2=2\lambda y^2=2\lambda z^2\). So \(x=y=z=R/\sqrt3\). Max volume \(V=8(R/\sqrt3)^3=\dfrac{8R^3}{3\sqrt3}\).

Minimize \(f(\mathbf{x})\) s.t. \(g_i(\mathbf{x})\le0\). Use the integral test to determine convergence of \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty\dfrac{1}{n^p}\).

Solution

Integral test: For \(f(x)=1/x^p\) (positive, decreasing for \(x\ge1\)), \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^p}\) and \(\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}\) converge or diverge together. \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}dx=\left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^\infty\) converges iff \(1-p<0\), i.e., \(p>1\). Conclusion: \(\sum 1/n^p\) converges for \(p>1\), diverges for \(p\le1\). (At \(p=1\): harmonic series diverges; at \(p=2\): sum \(=\pi^2/6\).)

PYQ 2010 — Euler-Mascheroni Constant

Define the Euler-Mascheroni constant \(\gamma\) and show it exists.

Solution

Define \(\gamma_n=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}-\ln n\). Existence: (i) \(\gamma_n>0\): since \(\frac{1}{k}>\int_k^{k+1}\frac{dt}{t}\), summing gives \(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{k}>\ln(n+1)>\ln n\). (ii) \(\gamma_n\) is decreasing: \(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_n=\frac{1}{n+1}-\ln(n+1)+\ln n=\frac{1}{n+1}-\ln(1+1/n)\le0\) (since \(\ln(1+x)\ge x/(1+x)\)). A decreasing sequence bounded below converges. \(\gamma\approx0.5772\ldots\) \(\blacksquare\)

PYQ 2009 — Asymptotic Expansion

Explain the concept of an asymptotic expansion \(f(x)\sim\sum a_n/x^n\) as \(x\to\infty\).

Solution

\(f(x)\sim\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{a_n}{x^n}\) as \(x\to\infty\) means: for each fixed \(N\), \(x^N\left[f(x)-\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{a_n}{x^n}\right]\to0\) as \(x\to\infty\). Key point: the series need not converge — it is an asymptotic expansion if each partial sum is a progressively better approximation. Example: \(\Gamma(x+1)\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}(x/e)^x\left(1+\frac{1}{12x}+\frac{1}{288x^2}-\cdots\right)\) (Stirling's asymptotic expansion). The series diverges but truncating at any term gives a good approximation for large \(x\).

PYQ 2008 — Bernoulli and Euler Numbers

Define Bernoulli numbers \(B_n\) via the generating function and give the first few values.

Solution

Bernoulli numbers are defined by \(\dfrac{t}{e^t-1}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty B_n\frac{t^n}{n!}\) for \(|t|<2\pi\). First few: \(B_0=1,B_1=-1/2,B_2=1/6,B_3=0,B_4=-1/30,B_6=1/42,\ldots\) (odd \(B_n=0\) for \(n\ge3\)). Applications: \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{1}{k^{2n}}=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}(2\pi)^{2n}B_{2n}}{2(2n)!}\) (e.g., \(\sum 1/k^2=\pi^2/6\) from \(B_2=1/6\)). Euler numbers \(E_n\) arise from \(\text{sech}(t)=\sum E_n t^n/n!\): \(E_0=1,E_2=-1,E_4=5,\ldots\)
